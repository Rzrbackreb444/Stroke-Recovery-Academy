# Market size

**As of 2026-09-05; estimates are explicitly labeled.** The addressable population is large but not equivalent to obtainable revenue. CDC FastStats reports 7.8 million U.S. adults who had ever had a stroke (3.1%), based on the 2018 National Health Interview Survey; it also reports 166,852 stroke deaths in 2024 and 2.2 million physician-office visits with stroke as the primary diagnosis (2016). These dates and denominators differ, so they should not be added together or treated as current active rehabilitation demand. The American Heart Association's 2024 statistics report is a useful corroborating clinical source, but prevalence, incidence, survivor need, and paying users remain different populations.

Commercial market reports provide directional context only. Two 2025 estimates put the **global stroke-rehabilitation market** around $317.0M (Precedence Research) or $320.7M in 2024 (Market.us), with forecasts around $817.1M or $773.3M by 2034. These are vendor estimates with undisclosed/variable category definitions, geographies, and inclusion of equipment, clinical services, or software. They are not a forecast of this platform's revenue and should not be presented as obtainable market size.

# Business models

**Direct-to-consumer subscription (lowest reimbursement burden, weakest willingness-to-pay evidence).** A free core with an optional low-priced paid tier is the most survivor-first starting point: accessible exercises, education, reminders, and caregiver coordination can remain available while paid features fund personalization, reporting, and support. A 2024 peer-reviewed Hong Kong survey of 577 adults found 58.9% willing to pay something for a health app, but median willingness to pay was HK$50 (about US$6.50 at the paper's conversion), 41.1% were unwilling to pay, and 90.1% had never previously paid for a health app. This is not a U.S. stroke-survivor sample and uses hypothetical self-report, so it is a constraint signal rather than a price recommendation.

**Provider/clinic SaaS.** Sell workflow value to outpatient therapy groups, stroke programs, home-health agencies, or accountable-care organizations: assigned home programs, adherence summaries, patient-reported outcomes, and caregiver communication. Reimbursement does not automatically pay for an app; providers need integration, documentation, privacy/security, clinical governance, and evidence that the product saves staff time or improves outcomes. Per-clinic or per-active-patient contracts are more realistic than billing every survivor directly.

**Payer/employer/health-system contracts.** A payer may fund the platform when it can demonstrate reduced avoidable utilization, better therapy adherence, or earlier escalation of deterioration. Commercial and Medicaid coverage varies by payer and state. CMS telehealth rules and codes change; the rehabilitation telehealth literature notes that services must be reasonable and necessary, and many telehealth services require real-time two-way audio/video. A standalone asynchronous recovery companion should not claim Medicare reimbursement merely because telehealth visits can be reimbursed.

**Clinician-supported hybrid.** A licensed therapist, health coach, or navigator layer can support a higher price and stronger outcomes, but introduces staffing cost, licensure, clinical liability, and state-by-state operations. It is a potential premium pilot model, not a scalable software-only assumption.

# Realistic monetization

Use pricing as hypotheses to test, not established market facts:

- Free access for essential recovery education and low-risk tracking.
- A low single-digit to low-teens monthly consumer tier only after measuring retention and demonstrated value; the WTP study's median is a warning against assuming $30–$50/month consumer conversion.
- Provider contracts priced against staff time saved and active caseload, with procurement and security costs included.
- Paid clinical services only where a qualified professional is actually delivering the service; do not disguise clinical care as an app subscription.
- Grants, hospital pilots, and foundation sponsorship can subsidize access during evidence-building, but are not recurring product revenue.

Avoid advertising based on sensitive recovery data, selling identifiable health data, outcome guarantees, and charging survivors for access to safety-critical escalation. Revenue should preserve accessibility, disclose the product's non-diagnostic limits, and include financial-assistance or sponsored access.

# Scale difficulty

Meaningful scale is difficult because stroke is heterogeneous (motor, speech, cognition, vision, mood, severity, and caregiver context), engagement often falls after discharge, and many survivors have accessibility, fatigue, aphasia, or technology barriers. Distribution is also fragmented: neurologists, rehabilitation therapists, hospitals, home health, payers, and caregivers each influence adoption, while the survivor may not control payment. Clinical trust requires evidence and safe escalation rather than generic wellness claims. Integrations, HIPAA/security expectations, accessibility, consent, and support for caregivers add cost before revenue.

A responsible scale thesis is therefore **distribution-led and evidence-led**: start with a narrow recovery workflow and a channel partner, prove activation/retention and a patient-important outcome, then expand. A realistic early objective is a sustainable niche business or provider-sponsored network, not converting the CDC's 7.8M historical survivors into paying subscribers. Treat any multi-million-dollar revenue scenario as a modeled outcome requiring explicit assumptions about eligible lives, reach, activation, retention, price, payer mix, and acquisition cost.
