# CHAPTER 5 — The Day My Son Was Born

> *"April 24, 2019. The day my son was born. The day I took my own first breath back."*

---

## The Wheelchair in the Maternity Ward

I had pictured the day my son would be born a hundred different ways.

In every one of those pictures, I was standing.

In every one of those pictures, I was the one who carried the car seat in. I was the one who held Lisa's hand while she pushed. I was the one who stepped forward at the exact moment the doctor said *"and here he is,"* and I was the one whose arms reached down to receive him first.

In none of those pictures was I in a wheelchair.

That was the version of fatherhood I had grown up watching. My dad did not sit when his sons were born. My uncles did not sit. Men in my family stood for the births of their children the way men in other families stand for a national anthem. You stand because the moment is bigger than your legs. You stand because what is happening in front of you deserves the whole of you, and the whole of you is upright.

On April 24, 2019, I sat.

I sat in a hospital wheelchair with locked brakes, parked at an angle the nurses had cleared so I would not be in the way of the cart, the monitors, the ultrasound, the IV stand, or the obstetrician. I sat with my left arm pinned uselessly to the armrest by gravity and a misfiring nervous system. I sat with my mouth dry, my throat half-numb, my words slurring out in the same crooked rhythm they had slurred in for the past five months. I sat because I could not stand.

The nurses navigated around me like I was furniture. They were not unkind. They were busy. There was a woman in front of them in stirrups who needed every set of hands in the room, and the man in the corner — the husband, the father, the chair occupant — was a logistical detail. *Sir, can you scoot back.* *Sir, watch your foot.* *Sir, we're going to need this aisle clear.* Sir. Sir. Sir.

I have never felt smaller in my entire life than I did in those first ninety minutes of my son's birth.

I watched Lisa breathe. I watched her grip the side rail. I watched her face do the thing faces do when a body is doing something a body cannot quite believe it is doing, and I watched it from a chair, and I could not get up to put my hand on her shoulder.

I watched my wife become a mother from four feet away.

If you have ever wanted to be useful to someone you love and discovered, in the exact moment they needed you most, that your body had filed for divorce — you know what the maternity ward felt like that morning. It felt like the worst version of myself was the version that was scheduled to meet my son.

And then he was born.

---

## The Defibrillator

He came into the world in a way I have never been able to describe accurately, and I have tried.

The closest I have ever gotten is this: there is a horizon line you do not know exists until somebody crosses it.

For the first ninety minutes I had been watching Lisa's belly move and the nurses move and the obstetrician's gloved hands move, and all of it was happening behind a kind of geographic line — the edge of the bed, the rise of the sheet, the lip of the world I was not standing tall enough to see over. I was below the horizon. The horizon was where life was happening. I was in the chair, looking up at it.

And then a small, pale, slick, perfect human body poked up over Lisa's horizon and into the air, and the air filled my lungs at the exact moment it filled his, and the chair I was in stopped existing.

I did not cry. I should have. People expect men in those moments to cry, and I am not above tears — I have cried in plenty of places I am not proud of. But in that particular moment my eyes were doing something different. They were not making water. They were making a decision.

The decision did not arrive in words. The decision arrived in the body, the way a defibrillator arrives in a chest. One second the rhythm is wrong. The next second the rhythm is right. Nothing in between.

What hit me was not joy. Joy was downstream of it. What hit me first was *purpose,* and purpose felt nothing like the soft, warm, greeting-card thing I had assumed it would feel like. Purpose felt like a hammer.

It hit me in the chest and it said: *you are not allowed to stay in this chair.*

It hit me again and it said: *the version of you that was scheduled to be his father starts now.*

It hit me a third time, and the third time it did not say anything. It just stood me up inside.

The chair was still there. My legs were still useless. My left arm was still pinned. From the outside, nothing about me had moved. But inside, I had stood up for the first time since the morning of my stroke, and once I stood up inside, the rest of the rebuild became a question of when, not if.

---

## The Promise

The nurse asked if I would like to hold him.

I said yes.

I will be honest with you about how that went.

She placed my son into the crook of my right arm — my good arm, my only working arm — and she helped me cradle his head with my fingers. My left arm sat in my lap like luggage. I held seven pounds of new human against my chest with one functional limb and a will that was suddenly larger than my body could contain.

I looked down at his face — wrinkled, pink, eyes closed, the smallest mouth I had ever seen working its small mouth-shapes against the air — and I made him three promises. I made them out loud, slurring, in a voice I barely recognized as mine. The nurses looked away politely. Lisa was still being attended to. It was just me, the chair, the boy, and three sentences.

The first promise was: *I will be the best father possible.*

The second promise was: *I will hold you with both arms.*

The third promise was: *I will play catch with you.*

That is the entire promise. Three sentences. Twenty-one words. I have repeated them to myself, out loud and silently, every single day of his life. Some days I have repeated them through gritted teeth in the middle of a rep that was not working. Some days I have repeated them in the dark at three in the morning when sleep was not coming and the doubt was loud. Some days I have repeated them on a good morning at the breakfast table while he ate cereal across from me with both his small hands wrapped around the spoon.

Twenty-one words. The whole comeback is built on twenty-one words.

If you are a survivor reading this and you do not have your twenty-one words yet — stop reading and find them. Today. Before you finish this chapter. Find the sentences whose violation you are not willing to allow. Write them down. Tape them to a mirror. Read them out loud. The twenty-one words are the load-bearing wall of every rebuild. Without them you are framing a house with no foundation. With them, the rest of the construction is just labor.

---

## Naming It

About forty minutes after he was born — after Lisa had held him, after the obstetrician had done the things obstetricians do, after the room had quieted from the choreography of birth into the warm, small noises of a family of three — I sat in that wheelchair next to the hospital bed and I named the law.

I had not named anything yet. I had not written a manifesto. I had not posted a single video. I was a thirty-six-year-old man in a wheelchair on the worst stretch of his life, and I had just become a father, and a sentence assembled itself in my head with the kind of authority sentences only get when they have been waiting in line for a very long time.

The sentence was: **chop wood.**

That was it. Two words. I did not have the science yet. I did not have the protocol yet. I did not have the framework yet. I had two words, and the two words were the entire doctrine.

*Chop wood.*

Do the work every day, no matter how boring, painful, or lonely. Do the rep nobody is going to clap for. Do the visualization when you cannot move. Do the mouth exercise when you cannot speak. Do the weight shift when you cannot stand. Do the small thing today, because the small thing today, multiplied by the discipline of doing it again tomorrow, is the only mechanism by which a forest gets cleared, a winter gets survived, or a body gets rebuilt.

I had grown up watching my grandfather chop wood in Arkansas. He was a quiet man, a builder, a person who said almost nothing but produced almost everything. I never once heard him give a motivational speech. I never once heard him tell anyone how to live. What I did hear, year after year after year, was the same sound at the same hour every morning: the dull, rhythmic *thunk* of an axe meeting a log in the cold air outside the kitchen window.

He did not chop wood when he felt like it. He did not chop wood when the weather was good. He did not chop wood as a special occasion. He chopped wood because winter was coming, and winter does not negotiate with men who waited for inspiration. By the time the cold arrived, the woodpile was already there. The pile was the proof that the man had chopped.

In the maternity ward, with my son seven pounds heavy against my chest and twenty-one words still wet on my lips, I understood for the first time in my life what my grandfather had actually been doing.

He had been building a future he could not yet see, in the only currency the future accepts: today's work.

I was about to build a future I could not yet see — the future where a one-armed man becomes a two-armed father — in the only currency that future accepts.

The chair did not matter. The arm did not matter. The slur did not matter. None of the equipment mattered. The only thing that mattered was whether I would, in fact, swing the axe today, and again tomorrow, and again the morning after that, until the woodpile became the proof.

---

## Why > How

There is a lie that gets told to people in catastrophe, and the lie sounds compassionate, but the lie is one of the cruelest things a survivor can be handed.

The lie is: *focus on how you'll get better.*

The truth is: nobody, including the survivor, knows how. The how is invented day by day, rep by rep, plateau by plateau, in collaboration with bodies and brains that are themselves making it up as they go. Asking a survivor to focus on the how is asking them to plan a route across a country whose maps have not been drawn yet.

The right question — the only question that actually moves the work — is **why.**

Once you know your why, the how becomes a series of small problems instead of one impossible one. You don't need to figure out how to be the best father possible. You need to figure out how to do the thing in front of you today, because of him. You don't need to figure out how to play catch. You need to figure out how to make your fingers close around something soft today, because of him. You don't need to figure out how to hold him with both arms. You need to figure out how to lift the dead weight of your left arm half an inch off your lap today, because of him.

Once the why is real, the how shrinks down into something a body can actually attempt.

I left the hospital that night with my son in a car seat my dad carried, with my wife in a wheelchair next to mine, and with twenty-one words and two words. The twenty-one were the why. The two were the how. The combination has carried me every single day for seven years, and it is the combination I am handing to you now, no matter what your catastrophe is, no matter what chair you are sitting in:

**Find your why. Then chop wood.**

That is the doctrine. The whole book is just the long version.

### Builder's Note

> *Once you have a why, you can survive any how. The chair I sat in for the first ninety minutes of my son's life is the same chair I stood up out of, internally, the second I saw his face. The body took years to catch up. The decision took half a second. Find the half-second. Your body will follow the decision eventually. It always does.*
