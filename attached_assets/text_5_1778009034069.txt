CHAPTER 3 | HOME BUT NOT FREE

Leaving the hospital felt like freedom — until I rolled through my own front door and realized I was still trapped.

The same walls. The same furniture. The same photos on the wall.
But nothing about me was the same.

At the hospital, everything had rhythm — therapy, meals, rest, repeat.
At home, there was silence.
And silence is loud when you’re alone with your thoughts.

I thought I’d feel relief. Instead, I felt useless.

⸻

The Weight of Stillness

When you go from being the guy who can deadlift his own bodyweight to the guy who can’t lift a fork, your ego doesn’t just bruise — it collapses.

I was used to being in motion. Stillness felt like decay.

So, I numbed it.
I ate. I watched TV. I waited for motivation to come back.
It didn’t.

In a few months, I ballooned to 325 pounds. Every pound was a brick of guilt and grief — physical proof of emotional gravity.

I wasn’t just heavy. I was anchored.

⸻

The Psychology of Depression

Most people think depression looks like sadness.
For me, it looked like stillness — like existing without momentum.

Neuroscience shows that inactivity itself reinforces depression.
When movement stops, the dopamine system — the part of the brain that rewards effort — goes quiet.
No movement means no dopamine. No dopamine means no motivation.

That’s why getting up feels impossible — because chemically, it almost is.

But the reverse is also true. Even the smallest action — standing, stretching, breathing intentionally — reactivates those circuits.
It’s science’s cruel truth: you can’t think your way out of depression. You have to move your way out.

⸻

The Thought I Never Said Out Loud

I don’t glamorize this part. I thought my life was over.
There were days I imagined steering the car into oncoming traffic — not because I wanted to die, but because I couldn’t picture living like this forever.

But then something inside me — maybe instinct, maybe grace — whispered, “Not yet.”

⸻

The Moment Everything Changed

On April 24, 2019, my son was born.

I watched Nicholas Jr. take his first breath from the confines of a wheelchair.
And something primal in me reignited.

That wasn’t motivation — it was ignition.
Watching him live flipped a switch I didn’t know I still had.

Purpose flooded in where despair had lived.
My brain, once trapped in survival mode, suddenly had a reason to rewire.

Neurologically, that’s exactly what happens when purpose appears:
the ventromedial prefrontal cortex lights up, linking emotion and decision-making.
It’s the same circuitry used to override fear.

That’s when I started fighting again — not just to walk, but to show him how a man rebuilds.

⸻

Chopping Wood

That’s what I started calling my recovery: chopping wood.

It meant doing the work every day — no matter how boring, painful, or invisible.
Not swinging for the fences. Just swinging for progress.

“Chopping wood” became more than a metaphor — it was a neurological practice.
Repetition strengthens synaptic efficiency — the brain’s ability to send faster, stronger signals through repeated action.
Each rep was a swing. Each flicker of progress, a spark.

If I couldn’t move my hand, I visualized it.
If I couldn’t walk, I shifted weight until my brain remembered how.

Neuroimaging studies have shown that motor imagery (mentally rehearsing a movement) activates the same motor cortex regions as physical execution.
So even when I wasn’t moving, I was training my brain to move.

That’s what chopping wood really means: training the signal, not just the muscle.

⸻

From Numb to Noticing

Once I started moving again — even slightly — I started noticing again.
The hum of the fridge. The sunlight through blinds. The feeling of air against my skin.

Those sensations are part of what scientists call somatosensory feedback — signals that remind your brain the world still exists around you.
When you’ve been numb, those sensations are proof that you’re still human.

Movement brought awareness. Awareness brought gratitude.
And gratitude slowly rewired my perception of progress.

⸻

The Neuroscience of Purpose

Recovery changed the way I saw strength.
Before, I measured it in pounds lifted.
After, I measured it in persistence sustained.

When you act with purpose, your brain’s anterior insula — the area tied to self-awareness and emotional resilience — becomes more active.
That’s not philosophy. That’s functional MRI evidence.

I wasn’t chasing my old life anymore. I was building a new one.
And that’s when “rehab” became “rebuild.”

⸻

Freedom Redefined

Freedom isn’t what you get when you leave the hospital.
It’s what you earn when you stop waiting for permission to rebuild.

Every time I swung the metaphorical axe — every time I chose effort over apathy — I reclaimed territory from the part of me that wanted to quit.

That’s not recovery. That’s revolution.

⸻

Builder’s Note

Real freedom doesn’t come when the door opens.
It comes when you decide to walk through — even if you have to crawl first.

CHAPTER 2 | THE AWAKENING

Waking up in a hospital bed after a stroke feels like being reborn into a body that isn’t yours.
Every familiar thing becomes foreign — walking, talking, even breathing feels like a negotiation.

At first, I thought I just needed rest. That was arrogance — the belief that I could outwork anything.
But this wasn’t about strength anymore. This was about surrendering to a new system.

⸻

Learning to Move Again

Rehab wasn’t inspiring; it was mechanical.
Each day followed the same rhythm: eat, move, rest, repeat.
Progress was invisible to everyone but me.

A “good day” meant I didn’t fall.
A “great day” meant I stood for five seconds longer than yesterday.

Michael reminded me, “You’re not rebuilding muscle — you’re rebuilding communication.”
He was right. The brain wasn’t broken; it was disconnected.
Each rep was a message from body to mind saying, We’re still here.

Neuroscience calls this activity-dependent plasticity — the process by which repeated, purposeful motion strengthens neural pathways.
Every movement was code. Every repetition, a signal.

⸻

The Work Nobody Sees

Rehab tests more than muscles; it tests pride.
You learn humility in the quiet, in the small motions no one applauds.
Each failed step is a conversation between effort and ego.

Functional MRI studies show that intention itself activates the premotor cortex, even before movement happens.
Thinking about standing primes the circuitry for standing.
That’s why showing up matters, even when it looks like nothing’s happening.

Michael treated me like a builder, not a patient.
Every cue — posture, balance, breath — was another beam set in place.
And slowly, my blueprint began to take shape.

⸻

The Baseball Bat

One day Michael handed me a baseball bat.
“Hold this,” he said.

He stood it upright on the floor and told me to balance.
I wobbled, cursed, and nearly fell — but then I found rhythm.

That five-dollar bat doubled my standing time.
No equipment, no miracle — just leverage and focus.

Later, I learned why it worked.
By shifting balance through one fixed object, I was training proprioception — the body’s sense of spatial awareness.
That single adjustment reactivated dormant motor pathways in the cerebellum.
The science caught up to what my gut already knew: creativity heals.

⸻

The Mental Battle

Progress wasn’t linear.
Some days, my body cooperated.
Other days, it betrayed me.

Neurofatigue is real — when damaged neural circuits tire faster than the muscles they command.
That’s why even small victories drained me.

At night, I’d stare at the ceiling and think, What if this is as far as I get?
That thought is the quiet killer — the one every survivor meets alone.

But then I’d picture my son.
He wasn’t born yet, but I knew he was coming.
That heartbeat became my anchor.

Purpose activates the brain’s limbic system — the same center that regulates drive and resilience.
My son became my spark, turning survival into construction.

⸻

The Spark of Purpose

His ultrasound photo hung by my hospital bed.
When I wanted to quit, I looked at it.
When therapy hurt, I looked harder.

That purpose triggered something biochemical — a rise in dopamine and norepinephrine, the same chemicals that fuel focus and motivation.
Purpose literally rewires the brain to persist.

I wasn’t just trying to walk again.
I was trying to show my son what it looks like when a man refuses to quit.

⸻

The Shift

The day I stopped asking Why me? and started asking Now what? was the real awakening.
Pain stripped away everything false — ego, pride, control.
What was left was truth.

I wasn’t being punished. I was being rebuilt.
The stroke wasn’t the end — it was the demolition before reconstruction.

Every rep became a blueprint.
Every flicker became a signal.
Every failure became another line in the design.

⸻

Builder’s Note

Awakening isn’t about finding the light.
It’s about learning to build in the dark until it turns back on.

CHAPTER 3 | HOME BUT NOT FREE

Leaving the hospital felt like freedom — until I got home and realized I was still in a different kind of prison.
The world hadn’t changed, but I had.

I rolled through my front door in a wheelchair, surrounded by the same furniture, the same walls, the same family photos — but none of it felt like mine anymore.
At the hospital, there was routine. At home, there was silence.
And silence is loud when you’re alone with your thoughts.

⸻

The Weight of Stillness

Going from the guy who could carry a refrigerator to the guy who couldn’t lift a spoon crushed more than muscle — it crushed identity.
I had lived my whole life defined by strength. Now, strength meant sitting still.

So I stopped trying.
I laid in bed, eating whatever I could reach, watching TV until the days blurred together.
I told myself I was “resting,” but really, I was disappearing.

Within months, I ballooned to 325 pounds.
The body I had once built like armor had become its own cage.

Science has a name for this: learned helplessness — when the brain stops trying because it’s convinced effort won’t matter.
My neural pathways weren’t just idle; they were rewiring toward apathy.
The same circuits that once fired with adrenaline now looped through comfort and avoidance.

Recovery doesn’t die from lack of motion.
It dies from lack of meaning.

⸻

Depression in Disguise

People imagine depression as crying or breaking down.
Mine looked like quiet — stillness that stretched for days.

Depression after stroke is common, affecting nearly one in three survivors, often caused by disruption of the serotonergic pathways in the brain’s frontal and limbic regions.
That’s not weakness. It’s wiring.

But knowing that didn’t make it easier.
Every day was the same loop: eat, regret, repeat.
The mirror showed a stranger.
The world outside felt unreachable.

It wasn’t sadness that scared me most — it was numbness.

⸻

The Moment Everything Changed

Then came April 24, 2019.
The day my son, Nicholas Jr., was born.

I watched him take his first breath from the confines of a wheelchair.
And in that moment, something inside me broke — not in despair, but in ignition.

That’s when purpose found me.

When dopamine and oxytocin surge together — as they do in new parents — the brain’s reward system reactivates motivation.
It’s the same circuitry that fuels determination, and that day, it lit back up.

I looked at my son and made a silent promise:
If I couldn’t stand for myself, I’d stand for him.

⸻

Chopping Wood

That’s when I created my mantra: Chopping wood.

To me, it meant doing the work — every day, no matter how boring, no matter how painful.
You don’t rebuild a forest with one swing. You rebuild it tree by tree.

So I started chopping wood — metaphorically, and sometimes literally.
If I couldn’t move my hand, I visualized it.
If I couldn’t walk, I shifted my weight until my brain remembered how.

That’s motor imagery, a scientifically proven neurorehabilitation technique where visualizing movement activates the same motor networks as actual motion.
Studies in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience show that repeated mental rehearsal enhances cortical excitability — meaning the brain’s readiness to fire — by up to 30%.

Just like a boxer shadowboxes before a fight, I ran old football drills in my head: blocking stances, footwork, pad strikes.
Each mental rep was a blueprint being redrawn inside my skull.

It wasn’t therapy in the traditional sense.
It was survival with imagination.

⸻

From Numb to Noticing

Once I started moving again — physically or mentally — I started noticing again.
The hum of the refrigerator. The air on my face. The tiny tremor in my hand that used to mean nothing but now meant everything.

Neuroplasticity isn’t just about rewiring motion. It rewires awareness.
Every flicker of attention strengthens the sensory feedback loop — the cycle between perception and progress.

The more I noticed, the more alive I felt.
And the more alive I felt, the more I moved.
That’s when depression began to loosen its grip.

⸻

A New Kind of Strength

Before the stroke, I measured strength in pounds.
After, I measured it in persistence.

Strength was rolling out of bed when I didn’t want to.
Strength was refusing the easy meal.
Strength was lifting my son, even if it took every ounce of balance I had.

This was no longer about getting back to who I was.
That man was gone — and honestly, he needed to be.

The new version of me was slower, scarred, but more intentional.
I wasn’t chasing recovery anymore. I was building resurrection.

⸻

Freedom Redefined

Freedom doesn’t happen when you leave the hospital.
Freedom happens the day you stop waiting for someone else to fix you.

Each small act — every “swing of the axe” — reclaimed a piece of me.
I wasn’t broken. I was under renovation.

And that’s when I realized the next phase wasn’t about movement anymore — it was about responsibility.
If I was going to rebuild this body, I had to own the blueprints.

⸻

Builder’s Note

Chopping wood isn’t about speed.
It’s about consistency.
Every swing counts. Every spark matters.

CHAPTER 4 | LAW #1 — OWNERSHIP

“Nobody’s coming to save you — and that’s the good news.”

When the hospital doors closed behind me, I realized something harder than any physical truth:
For all the doctors, therapists, and family support, recovery was now my responsibility.

I’d spent months surrounded by systems — charts, protocols, scheduled therapy — all designed to keep me alive.
But staying alive and learning to live again are two very different things.

The moment I got home, the noise stopped.
No therapists. No beeping monitors. No progress charts.
Just me — the wheelchair, the quiet, and the weight of reality.

That’s when the first law of the rebuild revealed itself: Ownership.

⸻

The Law of Ownership

Ownership means no more outsourcing the outcome.

Therapists can guide you.
Family can support you.
Faith can strengthen you.

But the reps? The decisions? The discipline? Those are yours alone.

Science agrees: the brain’s motor recovery network depends on self-directed repetition — movement chosen by the survivor, not just performed under supervision.
Studies from The Journal of Neurology show that volitional motion — movement you decide to attempt — produces significantly stronger activation of the motor cortex and basal ganglia than assisted motion.

That means your brain literally rebuilds faster when you take ownership.

Nobody can rewire your neurons for you.
You have to send the signals yourself.

⸻

The Mirror Test

Every morning, I’d wheel into the bathroom and face the mirror.
The man staring back wasn’t the one I remembered — half a smile, one arm limp, one eye heavy.
But I forced myself to look.

I didn’t say affirmations. I didn’t try to “manifest.”
I said one sentence, out loud:
“This is your life. Nobody’s fixing it but you.”

Over time, that line became a ritual — a kind of mental recalibration.
Neuroscientists call this self-directed neuroplasticity: consciously engaging thought patterns that reshape neural circuits.
Each morning’s reflection strengthened the loop between prefrontal cortex (decision-making) and cingulate cortex (emotional regulation).

It wasn’t vanity. It was wiring.

And that’s the truth of Ownership — it’s not motivational fluff. It’s literal neuroengineering.

⸻

When Help Hurts

It’s hard for people who love you to watch you struggle.
They want to help — to lift, to carry, to make it easier.

But sometimes help becomes harm.
Every time someone did something for me, my brain lost a chance to learn how.

So I started saying no.
If someone handed me my shoes, I’d hand them back.
If they opened a door, I’d close it and open it again myself.

That wasn’t pride — it was practice.
The Hebbian principle says: neurons that fire together, wire together.
Every assisted action fired someone else’s neurons, not mine.
Ownership meant reclaiming every signal, even if it took ten times as long.

⸻

From Survivor to Builder

Survivor used to sound like victory.
But one day, I realized surviving wasn’t enough.

Survivors wait for life to start again.
Builders make it start.

A survivor holds on.
A builder moves forward.

That’s the pivot — the shift that changed everything.

When I started calling myself a builder, not a survivor, my brain started to follow.
That’s not just metaphor — it’s measurable.

Research from UCLA shows that reframing self-talk activates the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, reducing fear signals from the amygdala while boosting focus and control.
In plain English: what you call yourself changes how your brain behaves.

When I said, I’m rebuilding, instead of I’m recovering, something flipped.
I stopped thinking like a patient and started thinking like an architect.

The word builder carries direction, intention, creation.
It demands action.

And once I made that shift, every rep became construction, not desperation.
Every flicker became a blueprint.
Every day became another beam set in place.

That’s when ownership stopped being an idea — and became an identity.

⸻

The Hardest Truth

Owning your rebuild also means facing uncomfortable accountability.
Sometimes, we helped build the storm that hit us.

For me, it was stress, diet, and a refusal to slow down.
I wasn’t innocent — I was reckless.
That’s not guilt; it’s data.

When you take responsibility for the causes, you take control of the corrections.
If you helped break it, you can help rebuild it.

That’s not self-blame — that’s self-power.

⸻

The Daily Audit

Every night, I ran a three-question audit:
	1.	What did I build today?
	2.	What broke?
	3.	What am I going to fix tomorrow?

It wasn’t therapy. It was tracking progress like a contractor tracks a project.
Over time, that process rewired my dorsolateral prefrontal cortex — the brain’s executive control center for planning and evaluation.

Ownership isn’t a one-time revelation.
It’s a system.

You don’t recover by accident.
You recover by accountability.

⸻

Science in Motion

Studies from Frontiers in Psychology found that survivors who practiced self-directed recovery rituals — goal setting, tracking progress, and daily reflection — achieved up to 40% greater functional improvement within six months than those following only externally guided rehab.

Their brains adapted faster because they weren’t waiting to be moved — they were doing the moving.

That’s the truth of Ownership:
The comeback doesn’t start when someone pushes you.
It starts when you push yourself.

⸻

Builder’s Note

Ownership isn’t guilt — it’s freedom.
You’re not responsible for what happened.
But you’re responsible for what happens next.

CHAPTER 5 | LAW #2 — MOVEMENT

“Motion is medicine. Stillness is rust.”

Movement is the spark that turns survival into progress.
It’s what separates the body that’s alive from the one that’s just existing.

When I came home, I thought rest was recovery.
But too much rest becomes decay.
It wasn’t until I started moving again — ugly, awkward, uneven — that I began to feel human.

Movement doesn’t ask for perfection.
It asks for effort.
And effort, repeated long enough, becomes momentum.

⸻

The Law of Motion

Law #2 is simple:
If you can move, move.
If you can’t, imagine movement until you can.

That’s not poetry — it’s neuroscience.

Studies in Neurorehabilitation and Neural Repair show that motor imagery — mentally rehearsing a motion — activates the same cortical regions as actual physical movement.
The brain doesn’t know the difference between doing and visualizing; it fires the same neural patterns either way.

That means even when your body won’t cooperate, your brain can still practice recovery.

The more you rehearse, the stronger the signal.
And the stronger the signal, the clearer the path for neurons to reconnect.

⸻

Starting from Zero

In the beginning, my progress came in inches.
A twitch.
A toe.
A tremor.

Each tiny motion meant something colossal: connection.
That flicker of life wasn’t random — it was neuroplasticity in action, the brain’s ability to form new pathways after injury.

Every repetition told my nervous system, this still works.
And every successful signal made the next one easier to send.

That’s how healing happens — one spark at a time.

⸻

Michael’s Lessons

Michael never let me coast.
He said, “You don’t need strength yet. You need signals.”

He was right. My muscles weren’t the problem — my communication was.
The goal wasn’t to build bulk; it was to rebuild dialogue between the brain and body.

Every step, every grip, every failed attempt was a message traveling across damaged circuits.
And over time, those circuits rerouted.

That’s the essence of experience-dependent plasticity — repetition changes structure.
Effort leaves a physical trace in your brain.

Michael didn’t treat me like a patient.
He treated me like an athlete trapped inside a rebooting machine.
And that mindset changed everything.

⸻

The Baseball Bat Method

One of my biggest breakthroughs came from something simple — a baseball bat.
Michael had me balance it upright, one hand on top, and shift my weight side to side.

It looked ridiculous.
But within weeks, my balance doubled.

Later, I learned why.
That single exercise lit up my proprioceptive network — the sensors in muscles and joints that tell your brain where your body is in space.

Using a simple tool increased sensory feedback, helping my brain remap lost balance functions through the cerebellum and somatosensory cortex.

It wasn’t about the bat.
It was about sending the right data.
The bat became my antenna — a $5 piece of wood transmitting a $5,000 therapy signal.

⸻

Momentum Beats Motivation

People always ask how I stayed motivated.
The truth? I didn’t.

Motivation is fragile.
It depends on emotion — and emotions are unstable.

Momentum doesn’t care how you feel.
Once you start moving, the movement itself becomes fuel.

This isn’t just mental — it’s chemical.
Each repetition releases dopamine and BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor), molecules that reinforce motor learning and increase neuroplasticity.

That means motion literally builds the brain that makes more motion possible.
It’s a feedback loop of progress.

That’s why I live by one rule: Never zero.
Even if I can’t complete the whole drill, I do a fragment.
Fragments still count.

⸻

The Science of Stillness

Stillness feels safe, but it’s sabotage.
When neurons go unused, they atrophy — a process called learned non-use.
The longer you stay still, the weaker your brain’s motor maps become.

Movement reclaims that territory.
It redraws your blueprint.
Every rep, every twitch, every tiny act of defiance is a vote for function.

Your brain is always listening — you just have to keep giving it something worth hearing.

⸻

The Power of Ritual

I made movement a ritual:
Wake up. Move something. No negotiation.

Some mornings it was stretching.
Other days, just standing.
But I never missed a rep.

Consistency, not intensity, is what rewires a brain.
The research backs it: repetitive movement triggers synaptogenesis — the birth of new synaptic connections — faster than sporadic bursts of effort ever could.

Motion doesn’t need to be perfect.
It just needs to exist.

⸻

Builder’s Note

Don’t wait to feel ready.
Move, and readiness will find you.
Movement isn’t what happens after healing —
it’s how healing begins.

CHAPTER 6 | LAW #3 — REFLECTION

“Honesty heals faster than denial.”

Reflection isn’t weakness — it’s inspection.
It’s standing in the middle of your rebuild, looking around, and asking:
What’s holding? What’s cracking? What still needs reinforcement?

Most people want recovery to be fast and cinematic — but real healing happens in the quiet spaces.
Reflection is where you stop performing for progress and start listening for truth.

⸻

The Mirror Never Lies

In the early days, mirrors felt like enemies.
They showed me what I’d lost — the asymmetry, the limp arm, the exhaustion written in my eyes.
But one day I stopped looking at the mirror and started looking through it.

What I saw wasn’t a broken man — it was a construction site.

Neuroscientifically, reflection is not just metaphorical — it’s measurable.
Journaling, introspection, and honest self-assessment activate the default mode network, the brain’s system for internal storytelling and perspective.
This network rewires the anterior cingulate cortex, improving emotional regulation and resilience.

In other words, reflection builds stability — inside first.

⸻

The Truth About Progress

Progress doesn’t happen in straight lines.
It loops, stalls, and sometimes walks backward before it sprints forward.

There were weeks when nothing changed — same drills, same struggles.
But when I looked closer, I realized the gains were hidden in repetition:
My grip lasted longer. My frustration ended faster. My balance wobbled less.

That’s why I created the Flicker Log.

Each night I wrote down every sign of life — even the smallest:
• “Left hand gripped for 3 seconds.”
• “Took 2 steps with no wall support.”
• “Didn’t fall during balance drill.”

Behavioral neuroscience calls this positive feedback encoding.
Recording micro-successes triggers dopamine release, reinforcing circuits of persistence in the striatum — the brain’s endurance center.

The Flicker Log became my scoreboard.
Proof that the lights weren’t out — they were just flickering back to life.

⸻

Reflection Without Judgment

The hardest part of reflection isn’t seeing weakness.
It’s not attacking yourself for it.

You can’t rebuild a house while you’re busy cursing the foundation.
The goal isn’t to feel good — it’s to stay accountable.

When I caught myself comparing to who I was before the stroke, I had to stop.
The question wasn’t Why am I not there yet?
It was What’s the next inch I can claim today?

Cognitive reappraisal — reframing negative thought patterns — literally reshapes neural pathways in the prefrontal-amygdala circuit, weakening stress responses and improving focus.

Reflection without judgment creates direction.
Judgment without reflection creates destruction.

⸻

Owning the Shadow

Reflection isn’t just about the wins.
It’s about facing the darkness — the nights when I wanted to quit, the resentment toward people who didn’t understand, the guilt for being angry at the wrong things.

Avoidance buries pain alive.
Writing it down digs it back out, where you can deal with it.

In trauma research, expressive writing has been shown to calm hyperactivity in the amygdala and restore healthy communication between emotional and logical centers of the brain.
That’s not poetry — that’s neurology.

The page became my release valve.
When I wrote about my fears, the fear lost its echo.

⸻

The Three Questions

Every night, I asked myself three questions:
	1.	What did I build today?
	2.	What broke?
	3.	What’s tomorrow’s target?

Simple. Brutal. Effective.

To Nicholas Jr., my son, my spark, my why. You’re the reason I chopped wood when I wanted to quit. Every swing, every rep, every word of this book is for you.

To Kasey, my cousin and one of the good ones — for being there through the chaos, for checking in when most people moved on, and for reminding me that family isn’t just blood, it’s backbone.

To my parents, Guy and Debbie, for always being there for me — the glue that’s held our family together through every storm. You’ve helped me pick up the pieces my entire life, and I’m the man I am because of the foundation you built.

To my sister Stephanie, for showing me the kind of love only a true sister has for her brother — and for being my punching bag growing up. You probably deserved better, but you helped build the fighter in me.

To Michael and the team at Encompass Health, who showed me that healing isn’t about perfect form — it’s about relentless form. You proved that progress can come from a five-dollar bat and a stubborn heart.

And to every reader who picked up this book — you’re part of the rebuild now.
The hammer’s in your hands. Keep swinging.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Nick Kremers, known as The Stroked-Out Sasquatch, is the founder of the Stroke Recovery Academy — a movement built to help survivors rebuild their bodies, minds, and purpose after life-altering setbacks.

After suffering a massive stroke that left his left side paralyzed, Nick spent years reconstructing his strength, speech, and sense of self. Through that process, he developed The Sasquatch Doctrine — a mindset and method that turns pain into progress and recovery into rebirth.

Drawing from both lived experience and neuroscience-backed principles of neuroplasticity, Nick teaches survivors how to take ownership of their comeback, one swing at a time. His philosophy merges toughness with truth — recovery isn’t about healing; it’s about construction.

Today, Nick leads the growing community at Stroke Recovery Academy, where he helps others “chop wood” daily, log their flickers, and find purpose through persistence.
