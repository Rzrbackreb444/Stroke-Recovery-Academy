Understanding Stroke
- Defining stroke and its impact on individuals and families
Stroke - A stroke is a serious medical condition that occurs when the blood supply to a part of the brain is interrupted or reduced. This deprives brain tissue of essential nutrients and oxygen, leading to the death of brain cells within minutes. The effects of a stroke depend on the part of the brain that was affected and the amount of damage done. Symptoms can include sudden numbness or weakness, especially on one side of the body, confusion, trouble speaking or understanding speech, difficulty seeing, dizziness, loss of balance, and severe headache.
There are primarily three types of strokes:

1.	Ischemic Stroke: This is the most common type of stroke, accounting for about 85% of all cases. It happens when a blood clot obstructs the flow of blood to the brain. The clot may form in an artery that’s already very narrow, or it can form somewhere else in the body and travel to the brain.
2.	Hemorrhagic Stroke: This type of stroke occurs when a blood vessel in the brain ruptures or leaks. This leakage of blood can put pressure on brain cells and damage them. Hemorrhagic strokes are less common but tend to be more deadly.
3.	Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA): Also known as a “mini-stroke,” a TIA is a temporary period of symptoms similar to those of a stroke. A TIA doesn’t cause permanent damage and is often caused by a temporary decrease in blood supply to part of the brain.
Types of Ischemic Strokes
Thrombotic Stroke: This type of stroke occurs when a blood clot (thrombus) forms in one of the arteries that supply blood to the brain. The clot may be caused by fatty deposits (plaque) that build up in arteries and cause reduced blood flow, or atherosclerosis.
Embolic Stroke: This type of stroke occurs when a blood clot or other debris forms away from your brain — commonly in your heart — and is swept through your bloodstream to lodge in narrower brain arteries. This type of stroke is often caused by irregular heartbeat (atrial fibrillation) which can lead to clot formation.
Types of Hemorrhagic Strokes
Intracerebral Hemorrhage: This is the most common type of hemorrhagic stroke. It occurs when a blood vessel in the brain bursts and spills blood into surrounding brain tissue. The cells beyond the leak are deprived of blood and are damaged.
Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: This involves bleeding in the area between the brain and the thin tissues that cover it. It’s often signaled by a sudden, severe headache.
A stroke doesn’t just affect the individual who experiences it, but also has a profound impact on their family. The ripples of this medical event extend to touch every aspect of family life, altering the dynamics and imposing new challenges.
Emotional Impact: The suddenness of a stroke can leave family members in a state of shock and disbelief. As the reality sets in, feelings of fear, anxiety, and sadness may surface. Seeing a loved one struggle with basic tasks can be heart-wrenching. Family members may also experience grief for the loss of the person as they were before the stroke.
Caregiving Responsibilities: Post-stroke recovery often requires significant lifestyle changes and the need for ongoing care. Family members may need to step into the role of caregivers, assisting with daily activities like feeding, bathing, and dressing. This new role can be physically and emotionally demanding, leading to caregiver burnout. 
Financial Strain: The cost of medical care, rehabilitation services, and home modifications can put a financial strain on the family. If the stroke survivor was a primary income earner, the loss of income can further exacerbate financial stress. 
Changes in Family Dynamics: Roles within the family may shift after a stroke. Spouses may become caregivers, children may need to take on more responsibilities, and the stroke survivor may grapple with their new dependent role. These changes can cause tension and conflict within the family.
Social Isolation: The demands of caregiving can lead to social isolation for family members. Friends may drift away, unable to understand the family’s new reality. The family’s social life may revolve around the stroke survivor’s medical appointments and rehabilitation schedule.
- Recognizing the signs and symptoms of stroke
Within minutes, brain cells begin to die. Early recognition and treatment can minimize brain damage and potential complications.

The signs and symptoms of a stroke often appear without warning. They may include:

Sudden Numbness or Weakness: This usually happens on one side of the body. It can affect the face, arm, or leg. For example, one side of the mouth may droop when the person tries to smile.
Trouble Speaking and Understanding: The person may experience confusion. They may slur their words or have difficulty understanding speech.
Vision Problems: This may occur in one or both eyes. The person may see double or experience blackened or blurred vision.
Headache: A sudden, severe headache, which may be accompanied by vomiting, dizziness, or altered consciousness, could indicate a stroke.
Trouble Walking: The person may stumble or experience a sudden loss of balance or coordination.
Remember the acronym FAST to help recognize the signs of a stroke:
Face: Ask the person to smile. Does one side of the face droop?
Arms: Ask the person to raise both arms. Does one arm drift downward?
Speech: Ask the person to repeat a simple phrase. Is their speech slurred or strange?
Time: If you observe any of these signs, call 9-1-1 immediately. Time is critical.

- The Crucial Role of Early Intervention in Stroke Recovery
In the world of medical emergencies, time is of the essence. This is particularly true in the case of a stroke, where every second counts. The period immediately following the onset of stroke symptoms is often referred to as the ‘Golden Hour.’ This is the critical window of time during which medical intervention can significantly influence the outcome of a stroke.

A we've learned, a stroke occurs when the blood supply to a part of the brain is interrupted or reduced, depriving brain cells of vital oxygen and nutrients. Without immediate treatment, these cells begin to die, leading to potential long-term damage or even death. The effects of a stroke can be devastating, leading to paralysis, speech difficulties, memory loss, and a host of other complications.

However, if a stroke is identified and treated promptly within the Golden Hour, the damage can be minimized, and in some cases, entirely reversed. This is why recognizing the signs of a stroke and seeking immediate medical attention is so crucial.

The importance of early intervention cannot be overstated. The sooner a stroke patient receives medical treatment, the better their chances of survival and recovery. Rapid response can mean the difference between a full recovery and long-term disability, or even life and death.

Emergency medical services are trained to respond swiftly to stroke symptoms. On arrival, they can provide immediate life-saving treatment and transport the patient to a hospital that is equipped to handle stroke emergencies. Once at the hospital, medical professionals can administer specialized treatments that can stop a stroke in its tracks and even reverse its effects.

One such treatment is a clot-busting drug known as tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), which can dissolve the blood clot causing the stroke, restoring blood flow to the brain. However, tPA is most effective when administered within three hours of the onset of stroke symptoms. This underscores the importance of the Golden Hour and the need for immediate action when a stroke is suspected.

In addition to medical interventions, early rehabilitation can also play a crucial role in stroke recovery. Starting physical, occupational, and speech therapy as soon as possible after a stroke can help patients regain lost abilities and learn new ways to perform tasks.

Chapter 2: Navigating the Recovery Journey
Understanding the Phases of Stroke Recovery
Recovering from a stroke is a journey that unfolds in several stages. Each individual’s path to recovery will be unique, influenced by the severity of the stroke, the specific area of the brain affected, their overall health, and the rehabilitation methods used. However, most stroke survivors will progress through three primary phases of recovery: acute, subacute, and long-term.

Acute Phase
The acute phase begins at the onset of the stroke and lasts until the patient is stabilized. This phase typically occurs within the first few days to a week after a stroke. During this time, the focus is on immediate medical intervention to minimize brain damage and stabilize vital signs. The patient is usually in the hospital, where doctors closely monitor their condition and provide necessary treatments. This phase is critical for preventing further damage and ensuring the patient’s survival.

Subacute Phase
The subacute phase of stroke recovery begins once the patient’s condition has stabilized and continues through the early weeks of recovery. This phase often involves the start of rehabilitation therapies. The goal during this phase is to restore as much function as possible and learn to manage any remaining disabilities. Rehabilitation can include physical therapy to improve strength and coordination, occupational therapy to assist with daily activities, and speech therapy to address communication and swallowing difficulties. This phase is characterized by rapid improvements as the brain begins to heal and adapt.

Long-Term Phase
The long-term phase of stroke recovery can last for months or even years. During this phase, the pace of recovery may slow down, but improvements can still occur. The focus of this phase is on maximizing independence and quality of life. This can involve ongoing therapy to continue improving physical abilities, cognitive therapy to address memory and thinking skills, and emotional support to help cope with the psychological impact of the stroke. It’s also a time for adapting to any lasting effects of the stroke and finding ways to prevent future strokes, such as managing risk factors and leading a healthy lifestyle

The concept of neuroplasticity and its role in recovery
Neuroplasticity, often referred to as brain plasticity, is a fundamental concept in neuroscience that describes the brain’s ability to change and adapt in response to new experiences, learning, or injury. This remarkable feature of the brain plays a crucial role in stroke recovery.

When a stroke occurs, it disrupts the normal functioning of the brain, causing damage to certain areas. This can result in a range of physical and cognitive impairments, depending on the region of the brain affected. However, thanks to neuroplasticity, the brain has the potential to reorganize itself and form new neural connections to compensate for the damage.

In the context of stroke recovery, neuroplasticity allows healthy parts of the brain to take over functions previously performed by the damaged areas. This process, known as cortical reorganization, can help stroke survivors regain skills and abilities that were lost due to the stroke.

Rehabilitation therapies play a key role in harnessing the power of neuroplasticity for stroke recovery. These therapies involve repetitive and intensive exercises that challenge the brain to learn new ways of functioning. The goal is to stimulate the brain’s plasticity and promote the formation of new neural pathways.

It’s important to note that neuroplasticity is a double-edged sword. While it can lead to functional improvements, it can also result in maladaptive changes if not guided properly. Therefore, it’s crucial for stroke survivors to engage in targeted and structured rehabilitation activities under the guidance of healthcare professionals.

Chapter 4: Rehabilitation Therapies and Techniques
 Exploring various rehabilitation therapies post stroke
Embarking on the journey of rehabilitation after a health event like a stroke often involves a multifaceted approach. This typically includes physical, occupational, and speech therapy, each playing a unique role in the recovery process.

Physical Therapy: Physical therapy is like the body’s personal trainer, focusing on restoring and improving mobility, strength, balance, and coordination. It involves a range of exercises tailored to the individual’s needs, from simple movements to more complex tasks. The goal is to regain as much physical independence as possible.

Occupational Therapy: Occupational therapy, on the other hand, is the practical guide to daily living. It aims to enhance the individual’s ability to perform everyday activities, such as dressing, cooking, and bathing. This therapy uses adaptive techniques and equipment to overcome physical limitations and improve the skills needed for daily life.

Speech Therapy: Speech therapy is the communication bridge, helping individuals regain and improve their speech and language skills. But it’s not just about speech - it also addresses difficulties with swallowing, a common challenge after a stroke. Through various techniques and exercises, speech therapists help individuals communicate effectively and safely swallow food and drink.

 Robotic-assisted rehabilitation and virtual reality therapy
In the dynamic landscape of rehabilitation, two innovative techniques are making waves - robotic-assisted rehabilitation and virtual reality therapy.

Robotic-Assisted Rehabilitation: This cutting-edge approach employs advanced robotics to aid in the recovery process. These robotic devices can mimic natural body movements, providing consistent and precise therapeutic exercises. They offer adjustable resistance and can be tailored to each individual’s abilities and progress. This technology is particularly beneficial in stroke recovery, helping patients regain control and strength in affected limbs.

Virtual Reality Therapy: Virtual reality therapy transports rehabilitation into a new dimension. It uses immersive virtual environments to engage patients in therapeutic activities, making therapy more interactive and enjoyable. This can lead to improved motivation and adherence to therapy. From balance exercises on a virtual beach to cognitive tasks in a digital maze, the possibilities are endless. This form of therapy can enhance motor, cognitive, and speech skills, offering a comprehensive approach to rehabilitation.

These emerging techniques are revolutionizing the field of rehabilitation, offering promising new avenues for patient recovery. They exemplify the exciting intersection of technology and healthcare, opening up a world of possibilities for improved patient outcomes.
 At-home rehabilitation
At-home rehabilitation can be a vital part of the recovery journey, complementing formal therapy sessions. Here are some practical exercises and activities that can be incorporated into your daily routine:

1. Strength and Balance Exercises: Simple exercises like leg lifts, arm raises, or balancing on one foot can help improve strength and balance. Always ensure safety by having support nearby, like a chair or wall.

2. Stretching: Regular stretching can enhance flexibility and range of motion. Gentle yoga poses or basic stretches can be beneficial. Remember to breathe deeply and relax into each stretch.

3. Fine Motor Skills Activities: Activities like buttoning a shirt, picking up small objects, or threading beads can help improve fine motor skills. These tasks can be incorporated into daily routines.

4. Cognitive Exercises: Brain-training activities such as puzzles, reading, or playing a musical instrument can help improve cognitive function. There are also numerous online platforms and apps designed for cognitive training.

5. Speech and Swallowing Exercises: Practicing speech and language exercises can help improve communication skills. Swallowing exercises can also be beneficial for those with difficulties in eating or drinking.

6. Walking and Mobility: If possible, walking around the house or garden can be a great way to improve mobility and stamina. Always ensure safety and have support if needed.

Remember, it’s important to consult with your healthcare provider before starting any new exercise regimen at home. They can provide guidance on what exercises are most suitable for your specific needs and abilities. And most importantly, listen to your body and pace yourself. Rehabilitation is a journey, not a race.

Nick’s Journey: Life After a Stroke
(0-3 months)

Greetings, world. My name is Nicholas Ryan Kremers, born on February 17, 1982, sharing a birthday with the legendary Michael Jordan. I’m an ordinary man, 41 years old, married but separated, and a father to a 4-year-old son. In high school, I was a football player and a social butterfly, interacting with people from all walks of life. I graduated high school with honors and proceeded to college. Although I thoroughly enjoyed my college years, I fell short of graduation by one class, leaving my bachelor’s degree in business administration incomplete.

The day that irrevocably altered my life started like any other. I was at a friend’s house with my pregnant wife. As I stood in the living room conversing with a friend, I felt a sudden pop in my head. A few seconds passed, and then I collapsed. Each attempt to stand resulted in another fall. I was utterly bewildered by what was happening. My wife, recognizing the signs, exclaimed, “You’re having a stroke! Your left side is drooping!” She declared she was calling 9-1-1, but I insisted I was fine and told her not to. Despite my protests, she dialed the emergency number. From that point on, my memory becomes hazy. I recall being on a gurney, giving a thumbs-up to a crowd of family and friends.



When I regained consciousness, I was disoriented. My head felt as if it had been invaded by chainsaw blades. My wife informed me that I had suffered a massive hemorrhagic stroke, and doctors had performed brain surgery to remove the blood clot. The clot was located on the right side of my brain, resulting in paralysis on my left side. They warned that due to the specific location of the stroke, I might not be able to recognize music or even distinguish a cheeseburger from a hotdog.

During my month-long stay in the ICU, I experienced hallucinations, believing everyone who entered was trying to harm me. It was the morphine causing these delusions. Once my pain medication was changed, I was fine. I had difficulty swallowing, so they attempted to administer my medication with applesauce. It usually took an hour to finally get it down. I was on a diet of pureed foods, which all looked like applesauce. I was so sick of the applesauce because of the meds that I couldn’t eat it. Consequently, I lost about 30 pounds in the two months I was in the hospital.


While in the ICU, they almost had to insert draining tubes in my head because my spinal fluid wasn’t draining properly. I remember one instance where I couldn’t move my head or eyes. It was as if my entire body had tensed up and frozen, even my eyes. I could see and hear EVERYTHING, but I just couldn’t move. The doctors immediately sent me for a CT scan. Everything was okay. A few hours passed by, and I unfroze, returning to my new normal: my paralyzed self.

When I regained consciousness and could think straight, I started realizing some things. I was going to have to live like this, at least in the short term, and possibly the long term. I could feel everything on my left side, but I just couldn’t move anything. This realization hit me hard and ran deep within me. I thought, “How long do I have to do this?” In the hospital, it was easy because I had everything done for me, but at home, I was, for the most part, on my own.

My wife was pregnant with our little boy when this tragic event occurred. She stayed with me the entire time, only going home to shower every few days. She would sit in the chair next to me, holding my paralyzed arm, and keeping my fingers straight so they wouldn’t curl up and get stuck that way. I had seen many stroke survivors walking around with one arm all curled up, so that meant a lot to me.

Since this stroke was so sudden and hectic, everyone was going crazy. My parents were furious because they couldn’t come back to see me. My blood pressure wasn’t stable, so my wife tried to keep the visitors down, because she didn’t want to see it rise due to company. There were a few fights about that. Eventually, my blood pressure went down, so family and friends were allowed to come see me. It felt good, being able to see people and knowing I could still recognize them. My brain was still there. That was a moral victory. Also, knowing I had such a huge support system, gave me confidence towards the trying time I was about to face.


Most stroke recovery comes in the first few days to months, so getting therapy fast was imperative. Therapists started coming in after I got out of the ICU. Their goal was just to see if I could sit up and balance myself and see if i had any movement at all in my affected leg. They would wrap the gait belt around me, maneuver my body, and lift me up. The entire left side of my body was limp, so almost the entirety of the work was on them. Also, speech therapy would come and give me tests such. I was having trouble swallowing, and it sounded like I was holding my breath when I would talk. They gave me a breathing machine I would have to practice blowing into for a certain amount of time. I also had some exercises where I would practice enunciating words.

After a few days of that, the therapists decided to try and get me to stand up and bear at least a little weight on my left leg. It was one of the hardest things I’ve ever tried. Imagine being able to feel everything on one side of your body, yet not being able to move it. That was so frustrating. After a few days, I was able to lift my left leg a few inches off of the bed. That was a major achievement because I could tell some of my brain was making new connections, and my body was coming back. I spent days after that doing those simple, yet incredibly hard, elevation exercises.




When I was first transferred into rehab, I was still pretty much out of it and hopped up on pain medication, but the part of me that was still there was pretty nervous. I still had lots of staples in my head, and wore a beanie, most of the time, to protect them and also to try and hide them from people. I’ve always had a problem with anxiety and worrying about what people thought about me. It’s pretty embarrassing going in front of people for the first time after major surgery, because you don’t know what people are thinking or saying about you behind your back.

The rehabilitation facility also allowed my wife to live with me. I really needed that at this stage in my life, because I was all confused, and just needed her by my side, although we had been having problems. I started off in a wheelchair full time, just trying to bear weight. We would practice weight bearing, pivoting, balance exercises, and various exercises just trying to get me out of my wheelchair. In my wheelchair, we would do heel and toe raises, leg lifts, and things designed to strengthen my leg, foot, and ankle. Those were very difficult because I was in a very dark place. I was basically still paralyzed on my left side, and felt there was no way out. I was trapped in my own body.

After therapy, I could go back to my room and watch TV with my wife. She would help feed me, and make sure I would drink all of my protein shakes, because I wasn’t eating very good. I spent about a month in rehab. That’s when I met Michael. Michael was a tall man, about 6’4", and 160 pounds, soaking wet. I thought, “how in the hell is this skinny guy going to lift me up and help me recover?” Keep in mind I’m 6'1", and lucky if the scale shows 260 pounds after starving myself for week. I could tell he was on the newer side of therapy, because it seemed every five seconds he was asking another therapist a question about me or seeking guidance to assure himself he was doing the best thing to maximize my recovery. I couldn't hate on that, because I could tell he cared, and I wanted to maximize my recovery, as well.  I’ll give you this, he was definitely dedicated and passionate about his craft. We worked and worked and worked. He would somehow support my left side and ankle, and we would walk around the gym. Keep in mind, I had no AFO on during this time.  Every time I would try and put weight on my affected side, it would turn, so we laid off weight bearing too much.
As the days in the rehabilitation center passed, the time came for me to return home. Fear gripped me as I faced the uncertainty of how I would manage in my own house. Thankfully, my home was a single-story building, eliminating the challenge of stairs. My father, foreseeing the difficulties I would face, had a wheelchair ramp installed on the front porch. He also fitted handles in the bathroom by the toilet and in the shower to aid my mobility.

The rehabilitation center equipped me with a brand new wheelchair, a shower bench, and a raised toilet seat. These tools were meant to ease my transition back home. However, when I wheeled inside my house, I was overcome with a sense of helplessness. I looked around, my gaze lost in the distance, and I found myself at a loss. I had only one functioning arm and barely a second leg. Even the simple act of eating had become a chore.

I spent countless hours in front of the television, eating, and sinking deeper into depression. There was absolutely nothing to do. I felt trapped within my own mind, my body a prison that I could not escape from. Getting out of bed was a monumental task, requiring the assistance of at least two people. My wife had to help me with the most basic of tasks, including using the restroom. I had a catheter implanted almost the entire time I was in the hospital, so holding my bladder longer than thirty minutes at a time was next to impossible. I would usually scream, " Lisa, I gottsta pee!," wait for her to let out a massive exhale, then bring my urinal in so I could let out my own massive exhale from releasing my bladder It was also a massive undertaking to get me into the shower, so she usually resorted to giving me a sponge bath. She would also bring me my toothbrush anfd toothpaste to brush in bed. I would squeeze the toothpaste into my mouth, brush,  and typically just swallow what was left.

One of the strangest experiences was not being able to wiggle my toes on my left leg. It was an eerie sensation, being able to feel everything, yet unable to move anything on that side of my body. This was my new reality, a reality I was struggling to come to terms with. But I knew I had to face it, one day at a time. This was just the beginning of my journey towards recovery.

From Wheelchair to Independence: A Tale of Triumph
For two and a half years, my universe was bound by the confines of a wheelchair, a device that has been a beacon of independence for individuals with mobility challenges for centuries. This marvel of modern engineering was both my ally and my adversary, serving as a constant reminder of my stroke.

My mission was unambiguous: to fortify my ankle to the point where I could abandon my Ankle Foot Orthosis (AFO) and walk unaided. This was no minor undertaking. It demanded countless hours of physical therapy exercises such as ghost squats, calf raises, knee lifts, side leg raises, and lunges. These exercises, recommended for stroke recovery, are known to enhance physical fitness, walking ability, and balance.

Despite the progress in my leg strength, my ankle remained a formidable challenge. Resolute to conquer this obstacle, I dedicated my spare time in my recliner to performing toe raises and foot circles at the ankle, alternating between clockwise and counter-clockwise movements. I would even wrap the elastic band around my foot and practice toe raises and extensions to cure my drop foot. After a few months of this rigorous regimen, I felt it was time to embark on the next phase of my journey.

I pushed my wheelchair aside and took my first unassisted steps. I moved from chair to chair, without the aid of an AFO, cane, brace, or any other support. The progress was gradual and the journey strenuous, but I was achieving it! I was walking unaided.

The path to recovery was slow, but every step forward was a victory. I began by walking from room to room, then from my house to my car, and eventually, from my house to my parents’ house, a distance of about 150 yards. The uneven terrain made the journey more challenging, but I kept my eyes on the ground, avoiding any pitfalls, and ensuring my safety.

After a few months, I felt ready to attempt a journey inside Walmart. Until then, I had been using an electric cart. I finally took the plunge, exited the car, and cautiously entered the store. It was nerve-wracking as I felt the gaze of everyone on me with each step I took. I was terrified of falling. I would grab a shopping cart for support and commence my journey. Step by step, I would quickly grab bread and groceries to shorten the trip and exit the store.

As the weeks passed, things started to get easier. The pace of shopping slowed down, and I grew more comfortable. My endurance improved, and I was able to shop for longer periods. If you’re feeling nervous or anxious, the best strategy is to immerse yourself in the situation and strive to feel comfortable. Despite my tension, I forced myself to go through the shopping experience, and I believe I emerged a stronger person because of it. I confronted my fears, as I always do, and emerged victorious!

Recovering from a stroke is not just a physical journey, but an emotional one as well. Stroke survivors often grapple with feelings of irritability, forgetfulness, carelessness, inattention, or confusion. Emotions such as fear, frustration, anger, grief, sadness, anxiety, and depression are also common. However, with perseverance and a positive attitude, recovery is achievable. Remember, every step, no matter how small, is a step forward. Keep moving, keep striving, and keep believing in yourself. Your journey is unique to you, and every victory, no matter how small, is a testament to your strength and resilience.

How Vlogging Saved My Life

My foray into the world of TikTok was rather unexpected. I used to tease my wife about her endless scrolling through videos until a thought struck me. Why not leverage this platform to document my recovery journey? Thus, I began recording videos on my phone.

My first video was a testimonial, a brief recount of my stroke experience, the challenges I faced, and my ongoing recovery process. It was a two-minute clip that set the stage for my TikTok journey. Following this, I posted a 30-second video of myself performing exercises.

The response was immediate and overwhelming. Followers started pouring in, and I received numerous comments about how my journey was inspiring hope in other survivors. This realization sparked an idea - by sharing my recovery journey on TikTok, I could inspire others while also motivating myself. It was a win-win situation.

As I continued to create videos, my follower count surged. Upon reaching a thousand followers, TikTok granted me the ability to go live. I seized this opportunity to interact with other survivors, answering their questions and sharing experiences. This interaction solidified my decision to fully commit to being a digital creator.

With each video I posted, my follower count grew. Upon reaching 10,000 followers, TikTok invited me to join their creator beta program, which offered payment for each video. This development sparked a desire in me to either return to school to become a therapist or use my recovery experience to become a life coach.

Motivated by this, I decided to create a nonprofit, website and social space for recovering stroke survivors called Stroke Lyfe Inc. at StrokeLyfe.com.The mission at Stroke Lyfe Inc is to hassist financially strugglig families in the aftermath of a stroke as well as help stroke survivors gain their independence by helping them modify their veicle to drive.I then filled out the paperwork and paid to make it official.  Then I filled out a form to make Stroke Lyfe Inc, a public, tax exempt, charity. I began designing and selling t-shirts, and also started dropshipping stroke recovery tools to my fellow survivors. However, this venture turned out to be quite a disaster. I invested about five thousand dollars in setting up the site on Shopify and hiring professional digital creators for SEO optimization. Unfortunately, I ended up accidentally dropshipping an Apple watch to someone using a stolen credit card, which resulted in nearly a thousand dollars in charges. A costly lesson learned!

Since then, I’ve discontinued the watch sales business and launched StrokeLyfe.org. This platform offers a social community featuring shared blogs, a message board, and of course, dropshipping of stroke recovery tools. I’m hopeful that this venture will be successful!

How I got my arm back
Regaining the use of my arm was an entirely different challenge. It all began with my shoulder, where I started with simple shrugs. The key to stroke recovery, I found, was consistency, persistence, experimentation, and working with whatever the stroke allowed. It was all about taking small steps and focusing on making a little progress each day, all while exercising extreme patience.

As I mentioned, my recovery started in my shoulder and gradually worked its way down to my tricep, bicep, and now my forearm and fingers. As my arm improved, I pushed myself and expanded my exercises. I would sit in my old wheelchair and practice lifting my arm over and over again, even while on the toilet! I discovered that as long as negative pressure was applied, the muscle would respond.

My caretaker, Karlie, was instrumental in this process. She would wrap an elastic band around my wrist, and we would practice lifting in every conceivable way - shoulder presses, bicep curls, tricep extensions, chest presses, and even wrapping the band around my foot to work on drop foot. I can’t stress enough the importance of fully utilizing your caretaker. They can provide connection and care in numerous ways. Karlie was incredible. She even went above and beyond her duties to cut my nails and shave me from head to toe, even in the most private areas!

Following this, I began making TikTok videos of myself lifting elastic bands with handles, which proved to be highly effective. I would lean onto the kitchen island and practice tricep extensions and pushup leans. I was then ready to attempt a pushup, but there was a problem; I had no strength in my hand yet. I couldn’t place my hand flat on the ground with weight because it would curl up. I purchased push-up bars from Temu and decided to give it a try. I found I could grip the bar quite tightly, but couldn’t release it. I filmed myself attempting a pushup. I managed to lower myself almost to my nose, then push back up. I did it! I decided to try another one, but failed and fell flat on my face. However, I had managed to do one! I then practiced these every day and eventually got to where I could do twenty in a row.

My hand was the most challenging part because it required fine motor skills, and the hand and forearm have so many different nerves to reconnect. I purchased a hemiplegia hand robotic training glove for one hundred and fifty dollars. It used pneumatic pressure to manually open and close my hand, hopefully forging new connections in the process. I would use it daily with my eyes closed, focusing on making new connections. After a few months of not seeing much progress, I sold it.

I then decided to try baseball bat therapy. I would grip the baseball bat and practice lifting it over my head and back again, working my shoulder and forearm. I then progressed to moving the weight of the baseball bat back and forth, working my wrist and forearm. This journey has been a testament to the power of persistence, patience, and the human spirit.
Outpatient Therapy
During a follow-up visit for my stroke recovery, I met with my neurologist. She was genuinely impressed by the progress I had made thus far. So much so, she felt there was no longer a need for me to continue seeing her. However, when she asked if I wanted more therapy, I didn’t hesitate to say yes.

I proceeded to attend local outpatient therapy. Here, they tested me in various areas, including walking with my eyes closed, looking up, down, side to side, side steps, and other coordination exercises. It was during these sessions that I discovered the use of wedges. These devices allowed me to stretch my leg and work towards completely overcoming my drop foot. I was so convinced of their effectiveness that I bought a pair for myself!

I also incorporated cycling on an exercise bike into my routine. However, despite my efforts, the physical therapist informed me that I still needed to wear an Ankle Foot Orthosis (AFO). This news was disheartening, considering the hard work I had put into avoiding it.

In occupational therapy, I engaged in a variety of exercises. I practiced squeezing a pressure gauge and spent time with a weighted wrist strap and stick, doing stretches, chest presses, and working on the hand bike. I also performed chest stretches in a corner with my arms raised, in a desperate attempt to regain the use of my arm.

This journey has been filled with ups and downs, but each step, no matter how small, brings me closer to recovery. The road may be long and challenging, but I am committed to seeing it through. After all, it’s not just about getting back on my feet; it’s about reclaiming my life. And I won’t stop until I’ve done just that.
Conclusion
As we conclude, let’s reflect on the journey so far. Today, I continue to create TikTok videos as part of my recovery process, serving as a beacon of hope for fellow stroke survivors. I have regained my ability to walk, albeit with a slight limp. I can even manage short bursts of jogging, despite my Physical Therapist’s advice against it.

Stroke recovery is not a journey for the weak-willed. It is a marathon, not a sprint, requiring patience, resilience, and determination. However, with the right mindset, no obstacle is insurmountable. The secret lies in maintaining a positive attitude and the motivation to engage in daily exercises. It’s crucial to rise, get your blood circulating, and commit to at least a small amount of physical activity each day.Consider starting a vlog and sharing your progress on social media. This not only inspires other survivors but also creates a sense of accountability for your own exercise regimen. This has been my strategy so far, and it’s one I plan to stick with.

Remember, things do get better, but the change starts with you. I have faith in your ability to overcome this, but the real question is, do you believe in yourself? Best of luck on your journey, fellow stroke warriors!
