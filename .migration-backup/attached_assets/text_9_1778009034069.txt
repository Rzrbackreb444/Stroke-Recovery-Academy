Chapter 2: The Truth About This Business

Let’s be real: laundromats are not “easy money.”
They’re steady money if you design them right.
They’re freedom if you manage them like a business, not a hobby.
And they’re hell if you skip due diligence.

There’s a reason so many first-time buyers fail — they chase revenue and ignore rent. They look at the quarters, not the cracks in the lease.

You’re buying a living, breathing ecosystem.
Every laundromat has four main organs:
	1.	Lease
	2.	Equipment
	3.	Utilities
	4.	Community

If one of those fails, the body starts to die.

Every chapter in this book comes back to one core principle:

Form over function. Structure over emotion. Design over guesswork.

When you treat your laundromat like a living system — not a side hustle — it rewards you with freedom, recurring revenue, and longevity.

Chapter 3: Location Is Law

You can fix equipment. You can renegotiate rent.
But you can’t fix a bad location.

If you ignore this law, the business gods will punish you every month with low turns and high regret. Location determines 80% of your success.

Here’s what to look for:
	•	Density: At least 1,500 renter-occupied households within one mile.
	•	Visibility: Easy in-and-out access from a main road.
	•	Parking: 1.5 spaces per washer — non-negotiable.
	•	Competition: Two or fewer competing stores in a one-mile radius.
	•	Demographics: Median household income between $30K–$65K is the laundromat sweet spot.

The CLEAN Principle

The Kremers doctrine uses C.L.E.A.N. for every potential location:
	•	C — Community Fit: Who are you serving?
	•	L — Lease Logic: Are the terms sustainable?
	•	E — Equipment Mix: Is it balanced for volume?
	•	A — Accessibility: Parking, traffic flow, safety.
	•	N — Numbers: Rent ratio, utilities, EBITDA.

Run these before you ever sign a lease.

⸻

Chapter 4: Lease Logic and Negotiation Survival

Bad leases ruin good businesses.
A perfect location with a bad lease is still a ticking time bomb.

Here’s what I’ve learned — mostly the hard way:
	•	Term: Minimum 10 years with two 5-year options.
	•	Rent Ratio: Keep total rent under 25% of gross revenue.
	•	Triple Net: Clarify who pays property tax, insurance, and maintenance.
	•	HVAC Clause: Make sure you’re not paying for the landlord’s equipment.
	•	Exclusivity Clause: Prevent another laundromat from opening nearby.
	•	ADA and Utility Upgrades: Confirm who covers compliance costs.

Always bring in a commercial lease attorney before signing — not your cousin’s divorce lawyer.

Part II — Building the Machine

Chapter 5: Equipment Intelligence

There’s no one “best” brand — only the best fit for your market and philosophy.
But you should know who you’re dealing with:
	•	Dexter Laundry: American-made workhorses. Built like tanks. Simple controls. Their Dexter Financial arm makes funding turnkey for new stores.
	•	Speed Queen / Alliance: King of reliability and ease of service. Strong warranties, huge network of distributors, and excellent POS integration.
	•	Continental Girbau: Quiet efficiency. Front-load champions, solid spin speeds. Slightly pricier upfront, but lower long-term water usage.
	•	Electrolux / Wascomat: Precision-engineered and great for upscale markets.
	•	Huebsch: Essentially Speed Queen under a different badge — parts compatibility makes it operator-friendly.

Doctrine Tip: Don’t fall in love with a brand — fall in love with serviceability and uptime.

Equipment Mix 101
	•	Large washers (60–80 lb): anchor your revenue.
	•	Medium washers (40–60 lb): most used machines.
	•	Small washers (20–30 lb): convenience and “quick wash.”
	•	Dryers: 1.5x total washer poundage capacity.
Balance is everything.

⸻

Chapter 6: Design, Layout, and Customer Flow

The best laundromats don’t happen by accident — they’re engineered.

Think of your floor like an assembly line.
Customers enter, load, wait, and fold — all without friction.

Golden Layout Rules
	•	Entry must immediately show washers in motion — energy attracts.
	•	Restroom in sight but not central.
	•	Folding tables by dryers — not washers.
	•	Keep your changers, soap, and seating in a triangle around visibility.
	•	Avoid dead corners or bottlenecks.

Lighting matters.
Music matters.
Clean smells matter.

These are your silent salesmen.

Kremers Doctrine: “Comfort equals currency.” A customer who feels safe and seen will spend more and return often.

Chapter 7: Funding the Dream

You’ve got options — some good, some ugly.

Top Industry Lenders
	•	Eastern Funding – Flexible programs for new builds, retools, or acquisitions.
	•	Dexter Financial – Ideal for equipment purchases.
	•	Clean Laundry Funding – Known for fast turnaround and flexible structures.
	•	Alliance Laundry Systems Finance – In-house for Speed Queen, Huebsch, and Unimac.
	•	SBA Loans (7a or 504) – Great for real estate-backed laundromats.

Funding Rules
	•	Always bring a business plan — no lender funds emotion.
	•	Keep your debt-to-income ratio below 40%.
	•	Show 10–20% liquidity.
	•	Prove operator experience — even if it’s from a mentor or consultant.

Doctrine Note: “If you can’t explain your business model to a banker in one page, you don’t have one.”

⸻

Chapter 8: Clean Operations — Efficiency, Maintenance, and Cost Control

You make money when machines run — not when they rest.

Efficiency Checklist
	•	Track your turns per day (TPD). Aim for 4–6 minimum.
	•	Keep dryers hot and vents clean — lint kills profits.
	•	Replace belts, seals, and bearings on a schedule, not when they fail.
	•	Control utilities:
	•	Water and sewer costs are your silent killer.
	•	Invest in high-efficiency washers and softeners.
	•	Monitor usage with smart meters.

Maintenance Discipline

Create a logbook for every machine.
Track issues, repairs, and replacements like an aircraft mechanic.
Clean means credibility — inside and out.

Part III — Scaling, Legacy, and the Doctrine

Chapter 9: Staffing and Human Systems

You can’t automate people — but you can build systems that keep them accountable.

Even if you’re self-service only, you’ll eventually deal with attendants, cleaners, or maintenance techs. Structure matters more than titles.

Attendant Structure
	•	1 attendant per 1,500 sq. ft. during peak hours
	•	Clear written task lists: cleaning, restocking, wiping machines, customer check-ins
	•	End-of-shift checklist signed and dated
	•	Bonus system for Google reviews and uptime

Leadership Mindset

Your job isn’t to do everything — it’s to design a system where everything gets done without you.

Doctrine Reminder: Leadership is architecture. You don’t micromanage bolts — you build blueprints that hold.

⸻

Chapter 10: Marketing That Works

Forget gimmicks — laundromat marketing is about trust, location, and repetition.

Marketing Rules:
	1.	Google Business Profile: Claim it, fill it out, and upload photos weekly.
	2.	Reviews: Every five-star review equals free advertising.
	3.	Local Outreach: Partner with apartments, shelters, or hotels.
	4.	Social Media: One real story beats ten stock photos.
	5.	Loyalty Programs: Offer reload bonuses or “10th wash free.”
	6.	Community Involvement: Sponsor school uniforms, churches, or first responder days.

Kremers Tip: Authenticity sells. Nobody cares about “brand tone.” They care about clean clothes and fair prices.

Chapter 11: The Laundromat Success Doctrine

The foundation of the entire system — simple, repeatable, measurable.

🧺 C.L.E.A.N. — The Five Pillars
	1.	C — Customers First: Experience drives retention.
	2.	L — Location Matters: Prime real estate beats big spend.
	3.	E — Efficiency Wins: Machines, utilities, and staffing must work in harmony.
	4.	A — Adapt & Innovate: Go cashless, add pickup/delivery, or hybridize.
	5.	N — Numbers Don’t Lie: Track ROI, TPD, and cost per turn weekly.

⚙️ W.A.S.H. — The Laws of Operation
	1.	W — Work the Business: Laundromats are not “set and forget.”
	2.	A — Align with Market: Know your demographics and price to fit.
	3.	S — Scale Smart: Upgrade slowly; measure before expanding.
	4.	H — Harness Trends: Sustainability, tech, and boutique models create margins.

⸻

📈 L.A.U.N.D.R.Y. — The Growth Ladder
	•	L — Learn: Master fundamentals before buying.
	•	A — Acquire: Secure location and financing.
	•	U — Upgrade: Invest in better machines and systems.
	•	N — Niche: Differentiate — self-service, WDF, delivery, or boutique.
	•	D — Deliver: Execute operations flawlessly.
	•	R — Reinforce: Strengthen brand and loyalty.
	•	Y — Yield: Grow profits, add stores, or sell high.

🧽 S.O.A.P. — Daily Discipline
	1.	S — Systems Check: Verify all utilities, changers, and machines.
	2.	O — Observe Customers: Watch how they interact with your space.
	3.	A — Adjust Operations: Improve layouts, pricing, or routines.
	4.	P — Promote Brand: Keep your story visible — online and in person.

🚫 D.R.Y. — Guardrails
	•	D — Due Diligence: Verify every claim before you buy.
	•	R — Risk Management: Insurance, safety, and compliance protect you.
	•	Y — Your Eyes Open: Never buy on emotion. No turnkey traps.

Doctrine Summary:
“Every laundromat can be measured, optimized, and scaled. The math doesn’t lie — your habits do.”

Chapter 12: Scaling Smart — The Multi-Store Mindset

Growth isn’t about adding stores. It’s about multiplying systems.

The 3-Store Rule

Before expanding, you should:
	•	Run your first store hands-off for 3 months.
	•	Have at least one trained manager who can replicate your systems.
	•	Document every process (opening, closing, cash-out, repairs).

Then — and only then — think about a second store.

Automation Tools
	•	Remote surveillance and coin counter software.
	•	POS dashboards (Curbside, FasCard, LaundryLux Connect).
	•	Online pickup & delivery booking systems (like Londr or in-house builds).

Scaling Wisdom: You don’t grow by adding chaos. You grow by cloning clarity.

⸻

Chapter 13: Selling or Passing the Torch

Every store should be built with the end in mind.

Ask yourself: if you walked away tomorrow, could someone else run your business — or would it crumble without you?

Exit or Legacy Plan
	•	Keep your books clean and verifiable (no cash in pocket).
	•	Maintain updated P&L statements monthly.
	•	Develop an SOP binder (operations manual).
	•	Build brand equity through service, not slogans.
	•	Know your store’s true EBITDA, not wishful thinking.

Your business should outlive your burnout.

Appendix C — Due Diligence Checklist (Larry Larsen Method)

Borrowed and adapted from Larry’s evaluation system (Laundromat123.com):
	1.	Inspect roof seals, ceiling tiles, vents, and water leaks.
	2.	Verify lease — bad terms cause more failures than bad machines.
	3.	Confirm equipment ages using serial numbers.
	4.	Review 3 years of income and expense data.
	5.	Walk competing laundromats and check vend prices.
	6.	Visit building department for new permit filings.
	7.	Talk to local police about neighborhood safety.
	8.	Evaluate ADA compliance and mold presence.
	9.	Research new developments within 1 mile.
	10.	Verify rent-to-revenue ratio (25% max).
