CHAPTER: ICU Hell
The lights didn’t go out completely that day — they just flickered.
December 3, 2018. I was standing there talking to a friend when my words started slurring. My left side dropped like someone cut the strings. Lisa yelled, “You’re having a stroke!” I tried to tell her no, but it came out broken: “Nooo Iiiiaaan noooot!” I tried to stand and my left side went completely limp. I collapsed. Perfect BE FAST. Right-side clot, left-side body gone.
They rushed me in. My family had the choice — helicopter to Little Rock or do the surgery right there in Fort Smith. Lisa was pregnant. She made the call for ASAP local surgery because my brain was still swelling bad. That decision saved my life.
They cut my skull open. Craniotomy. The surgeon lost the clot in the vacuum so they couldn’t even culture it. He pounded fifty staples into my head and joked I was so damn thick-headed he almost didn’t get through. I was supposed to be numb from the pain meds. I felt every single one of those staples like daggers driving into my skull. Pure hell.
I lost about fifty pounds in that ICU. Tube feedings at first, then pureed food that tasted like cardboard mush. Swallowing was destroyed. It took me an hour to get crushed meds down in applesauce. One night I woke up choking to death on the feeding tube. Lisa ripped it out — chaos, nurses rushing in. She saved my life right there. No blood with the tube, just pure panic and her not waiting for slow help.
Later the catheter crystallized inside me. When they finally took it out, it bled everywhere and hurt like fire. That was a different kind of pain.
Whenever Lisa stepped away, even for a minute, the morphine turned my brain against me. I thought the staff was trying to kill me. Felt like chainsaws buzzing behind my eyes. Terror.
Doctors looked at where the stroke hit and told me straight: because of the location, I wouldn’t recognize music anymore and I wouldn’t be able to tell the difference between a hot dog or a burger. They also said I’d probably never walk normally again.
They were wrong on all of it.
There I was, sitting in that bed on pureed mush, shitting myself, and singing “Oops!… I Did It Again” out loud every single time. Defiant. Broken. Still me. And in the middle of that humiliation I was still dreaming about winning Nathan’s hot dog eating contest someday. The same doctors who said I wouldn’t know music or food were proven wrong by a guy singing Britney Spears while taking a shit in a hospital bed.
That’s the part they don’t put in the charts — the part where your personality refuses to die even when your body is trying its hardest to quit.
Lisa was pregnant through all of it. She barely left my side — only went home to shower maybe once a week. Skipped meals. Held my pee cup. Gave sponge baths. Fought the system when I couldn’t. We weren’t romantic anymore. Truth is, before the stroke I had been trying to divorce her and I was an asshole about the pregnancy — made her prove it with a doctor because I thought she was lying to keep me around. I hated how passive she seemed compared to my strong mom. But she stayed anyway. She fought for me when a lot of people would have walked. I’m grateful.
That ICU was hell with beeps and fluorescent lights. Morphine, staples, tubes, hallucinations, pureed mush, singing through the shit, and the constant feeling that my own body had become a prison. But even in that hell, something kept swinging.
Builder’s Note
The darkest rooms are where the first flickers show up. When they tell you “never,” when your body betrays you, when humiliation stacks on humiliation — that’s the forge. Your job isn’t to feel inspired. Your job is to keep swinging anyway — even if the only swing you’ve got left is singing Britney while you shit.
Small Swing You Can Take Today
Find one small, ridiculous thing that still feels like you — a song, a memory, a dumb dream — and say it out loud or write it down right now. Even if it feels stupid. Especially if it feels stupid. That’s the first proof the lights never fully went out. Do it today. One swing.
